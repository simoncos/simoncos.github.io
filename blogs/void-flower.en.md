# The Flower of Nothingness

He dreamed he was curled up in a mass of black and white chaos.

The world was thick yet light, warm and soft. To use an awkward comparison: it was like the liquid of a fresh egg, roughly stirred but still inside its unbroken shell. The only two colours lay unevenly scattered within it, indistinct and without boundaries. In this chaos he was at peace, almost asleep.

If there were no light in the world, would colour cease to exist?

He had once visited a photography exhibition spanning the nineteenth century to the middle of the twentieth. In the dim exhibition hall, the black and white past clung to the walls, scene by scene. An old pedal-operated piano, curling Marlboro smoke, liquids that did not flow, and many more faces, blurred or clear, all lived within it. He moved slowly along the corridor, bending forward to study each photograph, but soon lost the will to do so—or perhaps the courage. Those scraps of paper connecting him to the past seemed to possess a viral magic. Through his senses, they released into the here and now a rich scent that had settled over a hundred years. Dazed, he turned his head, unable to tell whether those interwoven, lonely gazes and those bodies crossing like ghosts came from the phantoms left behind by reality, or from a reality as insubstantial as phantoms.

He went on walking through the labyrinth of the past, sensing that history, like this corridor, slipped in and out of alignment, full of chance and the unforeseen. Standing within it, he thought, with mockery but not without pity, that even here in the colourful present, those pasts could never become colourful again. All that changed colour, for one reason or another, was the paper that carried them. Like a coffin with its lid nailed shut: from the instant the image was fixed, from the instant light burned itself onto the negative, everything was destined never to change again. Was there any point in searching for the meaning of something that could not change? And these fragile things had come about by chance, had survived into the present by chance. To try to reconstruct a world that would never recur with a tiny, finite collection of points—where would reality be snickering?

There was no ultimate largeness, no ultimate smallness, no cause and no purpose. The real universe could not be bounded by the word “all”. It wore a cloak of such abundance; the emptiness beneath was enough to terrify.

More terrifying still was the past that could not be changed. Through the faint records of speech, writing, photographs and the like, people learned that the past existed. They discovered that the present could not trespass upon the past, while the past could always be reborn in the present like a nightmare. Once, he had gazed intently at the Shenlong version of the *Orchid Pavilion Preface*, Feng Chengsu’s copy of Wang Xizhi’s calligraphy. Character after character, uneven and angular, stretched its body freely across the paper. His gaze wandered and skimmed above the forest of brushstrokes. He felt that the ink had absorbed its maker’s life, and that he was caressing a transparent body. Those lines, thick and thin, hard and yielding, were nerve fibres tightening and relaxing, capillaries pulsing with force. Between them burst all the yearning and sighs of a life. What a delicate, complex, utterly soft existence. This was different from photography: it was not a flat imitation of the world. Once Chinese characters had moved beyond pictographs, what they carried was no longer a reconstruction, but a birth. If he could gently cut through that outer layer of paper, blood as black as ink would surely gush out. He imagined the liquid rising like a tide, washing over him, soaking and winding around his body, holding him in a tight embrace. Warm, and so hopeless. Was a life brought into being only for its death? “Those who come after will look upon us as we look upon those who came before.” Such was the nightmare: however many times it repeated, not a single existence could escape the cycle. The writing before him had achieved the semblance of immortality that countless emperors had pursued, madly and at any cost. Across the ages it stirred the same sorrow in those who looked upon it. Yet even a meaning so alive and beautiful would, in the end, be divided down to zero by the boundlessness of the universe.

Even the lament born of death would die. “Nothing” would defeat “something” in the end.

The thought sent a bone-deep chill through him, though he lay in a warm dream. He remembered another dream, a dark one. It was a world of total blackness—not the blackness behind closed eyes, but a blackness so pure that every description lost its power. The voices of family and friends echoed through that world, and he found that he could still remember the speakers’ faces. He was carried helplessly and without order through that viscous universe, so fast that the words in his ears were torn into shrill fragments of noise, as though a mangled tape were playing. He could sense the invisible, whirling traces of lines in the universe’s chaotic motion, neither simply curved nor simply straight. It made him feel that the universe was like a patch of ink on paper, already dense enough to be pure black, yet still being scribbled over. Everything was wild and without pattern, while on the surface it was a blackness that could not have been more stable. But even with those frenzied lines hidden inside it, it amounted to no more than the static crawling over an old television screen, or the bubbles that formed and burst in boiling water. Every point leapt wildly, yet none had any need to exist. It was like switching on a radio beneath a transmission tower: here, unmistakably, one received the most, yet all that was received, piled together, became the greatest loss. Everything was so incomprehensible—a disillusionment in full, extravagant bloom.

Darkness always made him feel that it held the richest, deepest existence. If he squeezed hard enough, surely something would come out of the sponge. Yet however hard he tried, he could grasp nothing. If the world had no end, then anything might exist, might it not? He needed a purpose in order to keep living. So he could only exhaust himself, groping blindly ever deeper, seeking an average in the rise and fall of all things, playing this game of probability with the universe: bewildering, full of loss, and impossible to stop.

Sometimes he thought that perhaps infinity was only the universe’s disguise, that the stars glittering in the distance were merely a magnificent sheet of stickers. It was like placing a mirror in front of him and another behind: space would extend as if without end, making the distance between him and himself, between one mirror and the other, fluctuate indistinctly between zero and infinity. Perhaps he existed only in this moment of thinking, and everything was merely a single record hard-coded into some file. Perhaps the universe had only a few motionless folds, yet convinced him that the world would go on changing continuously, without rest or end, like ripples across water.

He slowly woke, as though returning from a flight that had no duration and was so light it was almost floating. The universe outside the room seemed to have turned toward dawn. Newborn energy passed through the fabric of the curtains, seeping into a faint, pale white. This colour spread through the narrow room like mist, thick yet light. The room, too, was an eggshell: seemingly solid and round, but in fact covered with minute pores through which it greedily drew in air and energy from outside, surrendering what quiet remained in exchange. He tried to open his still-sleeping eyes. From every direction came the sharp, loud voices of the morning news; the symphony of countless engines and horns, driven respectively by the physical and chemical reactions of petrol and the impatience of drivers; and a humming that appeared in the city every morning without fail, filling its air, though no one knew where it came from. He often thought this hum sounded like a feeble, dying gasp, coming from an infant that had begun to age grievously the moment it was born, or from a set of gigantic gears, rusted and on the verge of collapse.

He seemed caught where the two worlds meshed, naked and utterly defenceless before this vast current of sound. The sounds kept reverberating inside his skull, stirring faint waves of dizziness in a consciousness thickened into jelly.

Had he woken from the dream, or was this another dream playing tricks on him?

He sat up against the headboard. The light in the room grew stronger, warm and soft, while his eyes were still hard to open fully. A dull ache stirred in his chest. He pressed a hand over it and felt something beating urgently through the skin of his palm.

Against a background of pure white, a blackness as dense as ink bloomed into a flower.
